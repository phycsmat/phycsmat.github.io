Solid State Physics

Crystal Structure

How atoms arrange themselves in solids — and why that arrangement controls everything.

← Back to Solid State Physics ~12 min read
Section 01

What is Crystal Structure?

Crystal structure describes how atoms, ions, or molecules are arranged in a solid. In crystalline materials, this arrangement is not random — it repeats regularly in three dimensions throughout the entire material.

This repeating order is what gives crystalline materials their well-defined, predictable physical properties. It is also what makes powerful tools like X-ray diffraction, reciprocal lattice theory, and band structure calculations possible.

💡
Why it matters

Ionic transport, electronic conductivity, mechanical strength, and optical response all depend strongly on how atoms are arranged. Knowing the crystal structure is the first step to understanding any solid material.

Section 02

Lattice and Basis

Every crystal structure is built from two ingredients:

Lattice
An infinite, periodic set of mathematical points in space. It captures the translational symmetry of the crystal.
Geometric
Basis
The atom or group of atoms attached to each lattice point. Adds the chemical content to the geometry.
Chemical
\[ \text{Crystal Structure} = \text{Lattice} + \text{Basis} \]

Place the same basis on every lattice point and you reproduce the full crystal. This decomposition lets you separate geometry (lattice) from chemistry (basis).

📌
Example

Copper (FCC): the lattice is face-centered cubic, the basis is a single Cu atom. NaCl: the lattice is also FCC, but the basis contains one Na and one Cl — different chemistry, same lattice type.

Section 03

Unit Cell and Lattice Parameters

The unit cell is the smallest repeating block that generates the entire crystal by translation. It contains all the structural information needed to reconstruct the full solid.

In three dimensions, a unit cell is described by six lattice parameters:

\[ a,\; b,\; c \quad \text{(edge lengths)}, \qquad \alpha,\; \beta,\; \gamma \quad \text{(angles)} \]

Here \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) are the lengths of the cell edges, and \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), \(\gamma\) are the angles between them.

🔑
Key point

The choice of unit cell is not unique, but the conventional choice reflects the full symmetry of the crystal as clearly as possible — this is called the primitive unit cell or the conventional cell.

Section 04

The Seven Crystal Systems

All crystals fall into one of seven systems, classified by the relationship between their lattice parameters:

Cubic
\(a = b = c\), all angles 90°. Highest symmetry. Examples: Fe (BCC), Cu (FCC), NaCl.
Highest symmetry
Tetragonal
\(a = b \neq c\), all angles 90°. Example: TiO₂ (rutile).
Uniaxial
Hexagonal
\(a = b \neq c\), angles: 90°, 90°, 120°. Example: Mg, Zn (HCP).
6-fold axis
Orthorhombic
\(a \neq b \neq c\), all angles 90°. Example: sulfur, olivine.
3 unequal axes
Monoclinic
\(a \neq b \neq c\), one angle \(\neq\) 90°. Example: gypsum.
1 oblique angle
Triclinic
All edges and angles unequal. Lowest symmetry. Example: kyanite.
Lowest symmetry
Trigonal
\(a = b = c\), all angles equal but \(\neq\) 90°. Example: calcite, quartz.
3-fold axis
Section 05

The Cubic Structures in Detail

Cubic structures are the most important in metals and many functional materials. Three variants exist:

Simple Cubic (SC)

Atoms only at the 8 corners of a cube. Each corner atom is shared among 8 adjacent cells, so the effective number of atoms per unit cell is \(8 \times \frac{1}{8} = 1\). Coordination number: 6. Packing efficiency: ~52%. Very rare (polonium is the classic example).

Body-Centered Cubic (BCC)

Corner atoms plus one atom at the body center. Atoms per unit cell: \(8 \times \frac{1}{8} + 1 = 2\). Coordination number: 8. Packing efficiency: ~68%. Found in Fe (room temperature), W, Cr, Mo.

Face-Centered Cubic (FCC)

Corner atoms plus one atom on each of the 6 faces. Atoms per unit cell: \(8 \times \frac{1}{8} + 6 \times \frac{1}{2} = 4\). Coordination number: 12. Packing efficiency: ~74%. Found in Cu, Al, Ni, Au, Ag.

💡
FCC and HCP are close-packed

Both achieve the theoretical maximum packing fraction of ~74%. The difference is their stacking sequence: FCC is ABCABC..., HCP is ABABAB... This affects slip systems and ductility.

Section 06

Coordination Number and Packing Efficiency

The coordination number is the count of nearest-neighbor atoms surrounding any given atom. It is a direct consequence of crystal structure and affects bonding character, stability, and transport.

SC
Coordination number: 6. Packing: ~52%.
Least packed
BCC
Coordination number: 8. Packing: ~68%.
Moderate
FCC / HCP
Coordination number: 12. Packing: ~74%.
Close-packed
📌
Why packing matters in battery materials

In layered oxide cathodes like LiCoO₂, the coordination environment of lithium and cobalt sites directly determines ion diffusion pathways and redox voltage. Crystal structure is not just geometry — it is electrochemistry.

Section 07

Why Periodicity is Powerful

The periodic nature of crystals makes the problem of describing a solid tractable. Instead of tracking the positions of \(10^{23}\) atoms, you only need to describe the unit cell — and periodicity does the rest.

This periodicity is the foundation of:

  • X-ray diffraction — constructive interference only occurs when Bragg's law is satisfied by the periodic lattice
  • Reciprocal lattice — the Fourier transform of the real lattice, essential for understanding diffraction patterns and band structure
  • Bloch's theorem — electron wavefunctions in a periodic potential take a specific form, leading directly to band theory
Section 08

Connection to Materials Science

Crystal structure is not just a geometric description — it is the physical origin of material behavior. Every design decision in materials engineering connects back to it:

  • FCC metals are generally more ductile than BCC metals because FCC has more slip systems
  • The layered structure of LiCoO₂ enables fast lithium diffusion between layers
  • Defects in crystal structure (vacancies, interstitials) are the key carriers in ionic conductors
  • Phase transformations involve atoms rearranging from one crystal structure to another
🔑
The central principle

Structure → Properties → Performance. This chain starts with crystal structure. Understanding it quantitatively is what separates materials science from materials selection.
