Crystal Structure

The atomic arrangement of solids and the foundation of solid state physics.

What is Crystal Structure?

Crystal structure describes how atoms, ions, or molecules are arranged in a solid. In crystalline materials, this arrangement is not random. Instead, it repeats regularly in three dimensions. This repeating order is one of the main reasons why crystalline materials show well-defined physical properties.

Understanding crystal structure is essential because many material properties such as ionic transport, electronic conductivity, mechanical behavior, and optical response are strongly influenced by atomic arrangement.

Lattice and Basis

A crystal structure can be understood as a combination of a lattice and a basis.

\[ \text{Crystal Structure} = \text{Lattice} + \text{Basis} \]

The lattice is an abstract mathematical array of points repeated periodically in space. The basis is the atom or group of atoms attached to each lattice point. When the same basis is placed on every lattice point, the full crystal structure is produced.

Unit Cell

A unit cell is the smallest repeating block that can generate the entire crystal by translation. It contains the essential structural information of the solid. When the unit cell is repeated along the lattice vectors, the whole crystal is reconstructed.

In three dimensions, the geometry of a unit cell is described by the lattice parameters:

\[ a,\; b,\; c,\; \alpha,\; \beta,\; \gamma \]

Here, \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\) are the cell edge lengths, while \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), and \(\gamma\) are the angles between the edges.

Why Periodicity Matters

The periodic nature of crystals allows physicists to describe solids using symmetry and translational repetition. This periodicity is what makes concepts like diffraction, reciprocal lattice, and band structure possible. Without periodic order, many powerful tools of solid state physics would not work in the same way.

Common Crystal Systems

Crystals are grouped into different crystal systems based on their unit-cell geometry. The seven classical crystal systems are:

  • Cubic
  • Tetragonal
  • Orthorhombic
  • Hexagonal
  • Trigonal
  • Monoclinic
  • Triclinic

Each system has a different relation between the cell lengths and angles, and this affects the symmetry and physical behavior of the material.

Examples of Important Crystal Structures

1. Simple Cubic (SC)

Atoms are located only at the corners of a cube. This is a useful model structure but is relatively rare in real materials.

2. Body-Centered Cubic (BCC)

In addition to corner atoms, one atom is located at the center of the cube. Metals such as iron can adopt this structure.

3. Face-Centered Cubic (FCC)

Atoms are located at the cube corners and at the centers of all cube faces. This structure is common in many metals such as aluminum, copper, and nickel.

4. Hexagonal Close-Packed (HCP)

This is another efficient packing arrangement found in many metallic systems. It differs from FCC in stacking sequence and symmetry.

Coordination Number and Packing

The coordination number is the number of nearest neighbors surrounding an atom. This is an important structural concept because it affects stability, bonding, and transport pathways. Close-packed structures such as FCC and HCP have high coordination numbers and efficient atomic packing.

Connection to Materials Science

In materials science, crystal structure is not just a geometric description. It directly influences diffusion channels, phase stability, redox behavior, and defect formation. For example, in battery materials, the crystal framework strongly affects how ions move through the solid and whether the structure remains stable during cycling.

Summary

Crystal structure is one of the most fundamental ideas in solid state physics. To understand solids, we need to understand how atoms repeat in space, how that repetition is described by lattices and unit cells, and how structure governs physical properties.
