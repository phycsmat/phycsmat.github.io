Section 01

What is Solid State Physics?

Solid state physics studies the physical properties of solid materials — how atoms arrange themselves, how electrons behave inside them, and how these microscopic features determine macroscopic properties like electrical conductivity, magnetism, and mechanical strength.

It forms the scientific foundation of modern materials science, and is essential for understanding semiconductors, batteries, magnetic materials, superconductors, and structural alloys.

💡
The big idea

Structure controls properties. The way atoms are arranged determines almost everything about how a material behaves. This is the central principle connecting solid state physics to materials science.

Section 02

Why Study Solids?

Solids make up the materials in every device, building, battery, and machine we interact with. The chips in computers, structural components in aircraft, electrodes in lithium-ion batteries — all of their behavior emerges from atomic-scale physics.

By understanding solid state physics, we can answer questions like:

  • Why do metals conduct electricity but ceramics don't?
  • Why is diamond harder than graphite, despite both being pure carbon?
  • Why does a cathode material degrade after many charge cycles?
🔑
Key insight

All of these questions trace back to atomic structure. Solid state physics gives you the tools to answer them from first principles.

Section 03

Classification of Solids

The most fundamental distinction in solid state physics is whether a material has long-range atomic order:

Crystalline
Atoms arranged in a regular, repeating pattern extending across the entire material. Describable by a lattice.
Long-range order
Amorphous
No long-range periodic order. May have short-range order. Classic example: glass.
No long-range order
Polycrystalline
Many small crystalline grains oriented in different directions. Most engineering metals fall here.
Grain structure
Section 04

Crystal Lattice and Basis

Every crystal structure can be decomposed into two parts:

  • A lattice — an infinite, periodic set of points in space
  • A basis — the group of atoms attached to each lattice point
\[ \text{Crystal Structure} = \text{Lattice} + \text{Basis} \]

This decomposition is one of the most powerful ideas in solid state physics. It connects geometry (the lattice) with chemistry (the basis), and makes the infinite complexity of a crystal tractable.

📌
Example

In an FCC metal like copper, the lattice is face-centered cubic and the basis is a single Cu atom. In a compound like NaCl, the basis contains one Na and one Cl atom per lattice point.

Section 05

Unit Cell

The unit cell is the smallest repeating building block of a crystal. Translating it in three dimensions reproduces the entire crystal. It is chosen to reflect the full symmetry of the crystal as clearly as possible.

Common Unit Cell Types

  • Simple Cubic (SC) — atoms only at corners. Rare in nature (polonium).
  • Body-Centered Cubic (BCC) — corner atoms plus one in the center. Iron (at room temperature), tungsten.
  • Face-Centered Cubic (FCC) — corners plus one on each face. Copper, aluminum, gold. Close-packed.
💡
Packing efficiency

FCC and HCP are the most efficiently packed structures (~74% volume filled). This high packing fraction affects density, ductility, and slip behavior in metals.

Section 07

Connection to Materials Science

Solid state physics is the bridge between fundamental quantum mechanics and practical materials engineering. In materials science, every design decision — choosing a cathode material, an alloy composition, or a coating thickness — traces back to atomic-level physics covered in this field.

For example, the crystal structure of a battery cathode material (e.g., layered LiCoO₂) directly determines how lithium ions diffuse through it, how electrons conduct, and how the structure responds to repeated cycling. Understanding the physics lets you predict and engineer the behavior.
