Kinematics

The study of motion, how objects move through space and time, without asking why.

← Back to Topics ~8 min read
Section 01

What is Kinematics?

Kinematics is the branch of classical mechanics that describe motion, how an object move through space and time. It deal with quantity like position, displacement, velocity, and acceleration.

But this is something that you need to notice, is that it does not care whether the object is being pushed by a force, pulled by gravity, or driven by an electric field. It onlt describe the motion itself, precisely, mathematically, without explaination.

💡
Think of it this way

Kinematics is the language of motion. Before you can understand forces, you need to be able to precisely describe what an object is doing.

Section 02

Key Quantities

Every kinematic problem uses some combination of these five quantities. Understanding the distinction between scalar and vector is critical.

Distance
Total length of path traveled. Does not depend on direction.
Scalar
Displacement
Change in position from start to end point. Direction matters.
Vector
Speed
How fast an object moves — rate of change of distance.
Scalar
Velocity
Rate of change of displacement. Speed with a direction.
Vector
Acceleration
Rate of change of velocity. Can be a change in speed or direction.
Vector
📌
Scalar vs Vector

Scalar quantities have magnitude only. Vector quantities have both magnitude and direction. This distinction changes how you add, subtract, and use them in equations.

Section 03

Core Equations

Average Velocity

Velocity is the rate of change of displacement over time:

\[ v = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} \]

where \(\Delta x\) is displacement and \(\Delta t\) is the time interval.

Average Acceleration

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity over time:

\[ a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{v_f - v_i}{\Delta t} \]

Kinematic Equations (constant acceleration)

When acceleration is constant, four equations relate all kinematic quantities:

\[ v_f = v_i + a\,t \]
\[ x = v_i t + \tfrac{1}{2}a\,t^2 \]
\[ v_f^2 = v_i^2 + 2a\,x \]
🔑
Key condition

These equations only apply when acceleration is constant. For variable acceleration, you need calculus (integration).

Section 04

Worked Example

A car starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at \(3\,\text{m/s}^2\) for 6 seconds. Find its final velocity and the distance traveled.

Step 1 — List what you know

  • Initial velocity: \(v_i = 0\,\text{m/s}\)
  • Acceleration: \(a = 3\,\text{m/s}^2\)
  • Time: \(t = 6\,\text{s}\)

Step 2 — Find final velocity

\[ v_f = v_i + at = 0 + 3 \times 6 = 18\,\text{m/s} \]

Step 3 — Find distance

\[ x = v_i t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 = 0 + \tfrac{1}{2}(3)(6^2) = 54\,\text{m} \]
✅
Always list knowns first

Identifying which variables you have determines which kinematic equation to use. This habit prevents errors in more complex problems.

Section 05

Why Kinematics Matters

Kinematics is the foundation of classical mechanics. Engineers use it to design safe braking systems, astronomers use it to track planetary motion, and athletes and coaches use it to optimize performance.

More importantly for this course — kinematics builds the habit of thinking quantitatively about physical phenomena. That same habit carries directly into solid state physics and materials science, where describing how electrons and atoms move is equally essential.
